1. OBSERVE — From Simulation to Reality

Real driving at Buttonwillow felt different from simulator practice: I could feel the forces and the consequences of each decision. During training, I became more comfortable with the car and noticed the importance of corner exits. After driving, I wanted to use telemetry to check those impressions and locate where my lap time changed.

5. DELTA TIME — Locating Performance Loss

Comparing cumulative time differences across the selected laps showed where the 7.401-second gain developed. Across ten equal-distance sectors of the approximately 4.64 km lap, the first three contributed about 4.86 seconds and the final three another 2.27 seconds, with relatively little change in the middle. Lap C was slightly slower in two sectors, reminding me that my fastest overall lap still contained areas to improve.

How to read this chart

At each distance along the track, the lines show how much later Lap A (blue) and Lap B (orange) reached that same place than the fastest lap, C. Lap C is the zero baseline.

  • Line rising: that lap is losing more time to C in this part of the track.
  • Line nearly flat: the time gap is staying about the same.
  • Line falling: that lap is gaining a little time back against C.

6. RACING LINE — Comparing Two Paths

At the uphill U-turn near 0.61 km into the lap, Lap C follows a wider recorded arc and carries more speed. The animation below shows the two GPS paths; the comparison asks whether the line could help explain the difference.

WORKING HYPOTHESISA smoother arc may help preserve speed through an uphill corner.

What the data show. On the approach to this turn, my two GPS paths are close. They separate through the middle and exit: on Lap C, the recorded path follows a wider arc, reaching about 5 m outside the Lap A trace around 0.64–0.66 km, then converging again. The logger reports roughly 1 m positional accuracy, so this is an approximate comparison of vehicle paths, not a survey of the pavement edges.

Lap C enters the 0.47 km point at about 113 km/h versus 106 km/h on Lap A. Its lowest speed in the turn is about 61.3 km/h versus 50.9 km/h, and at 0.78 km it is about 99 km/h versus 92 km/h. Over the 0.47–0.78 km window, Lap C takes about 13.71 seconds against 15.14 seconds for Lap A—a gain of about 1.43 seconds.

My interpretation. The GPS slope and within-lap altitude changes indicate a short uphill rise near the slowest point. Preserving speed as the car climbs may matter, and the wider path is one possible explanation. Entry speed and unmeasured braking and throttle inputs also differed.

What I would test. With my instructor, I would compare the paths at similar entry speeds and review synchronized video, exit speed and section time. These laps do not establish that the wider path alone caused the gain.

After driving, the AI-assisted path and speed comparison helped me develop my hypothesis that the uphill exit speed was related to the path. My coach had guided the line during training; the later visualization made its recorded shape and speed differences easier to examine.

The useful question became how entry, rotation and exit work together, rather than whether I could brake later at one point.

L6’s sustained deceleration begins about 13 m later, with a larger smoothed deceleration peak: about 0.68 g versus 0.54 g. Yet its minimum speed is higher, not lower. The minimum also occurs about 30 m earlier along the travelled distance, followed by an earlier recovery in speed.

L6 reaches C about 0.89 seconds sooner. About 0.79 seconds of that gain is already present at B, and the higher speed there carries into the downstream section. The gain is not simply a higher speed at one exit point.

The GPS paths differ at roughly the metre scale, close to the position uncertainty. That does not prove identical lines, but it gives me little basis to explain the gain as a major route change. The clearer difference is in how speed falls and recovers.

This comparison shifts my attention toward how slowing, downshifting and turning fit together. It does not establish a rule to brake later or harder: without pedal sensors, I cannot identify the first brake application, exact release or throttle timing. I would review synchronized video with my instructor before attributing the change to a specific input. Section 9 follows the related question of how an exit affects the next straight.

8. VEHICLE DYNAMICS — What the Acceleration Channels Show

Peak absolute lateral acceleration increased from about 1.09 g to 1.21 g. That measures a change in the car’s motion; it does not measure confidence or establish how close either lap was to the available grip limit.

The northwest U-turn comparison gave me a reason to study how braking and turning overlap. My sense of becoming more comfortable comes from the driving experience. To connect it with specific inputs, I would need video and more direct measurements.

9. FINAL CORNER — Exit Speed, Shifting and the Run to the Finish

During training, I noticed that a good exit helped me reach my usual upshift RPM sooner on the following straight. I wanted to know whether the data supported that feeling. Reaching that RPM sooner is different from choosing to shift at a lower RPM.

Testing my first explanation

An AI-assisted review of all three AiM exports found 20 final-corner passages. After excluding three outlaps and one slow passage without the usual final three-to-four upshift, 16 complete laps could be compared. This extends the three selected laps used elsewhere in this report.

The comparison starts at a fixed GPS gate near the corner exit and ends at a common finish gate about 250 m later. This avoids differences between the sessions’ beacon positions. The speeds below refer to these fixed locations, not each lap’s exact point of maximum acceleration.

The shift is visible; its benefit is harder to separate

On afternoon L7, RPM falls from about 5,920 to 4,441 in roughly 0.40 seconds, accompanied by a calculated change from third to fourth gear. Speed briefly flattens around the shift before rising again. This identifies the event, but the RPM-drop duration is not a direct measurement of clutch operation.

L3 shifts about 0.38 seconds earlier than L2 and reaches the finish about 0.115 seconds sooner. That initially fits my explanation. But L3 also starts this section about 1.9 km/h faster, so the gain cannot be assigned to shifting alone.

L7 challenges a simple “earlier is faster” rule: it shifts about 0.54 seconds later than L3, yet finishes the section about 0.135 seconds sooner. It starts faster and reaches a higher RPM before shifting. Across the six complete afternoon laps, earlier shifting does not show a clear relationship with shorter section time.

What changed in my understanding

The more consistent finding is that a higher speed at the exit gate accompanies a shorter run to the finish. A better exit gives the car a higher starting speed on the straight; it does not necessarily produce a larger acceleration. These laps do not isolate the independent benefit of shift timing.

The next question starts before the exit

This brings me back to the line: how do entry position, the car’s direction and the path through a corner affect where I can begin sustained acceleration? The uphill U-turn in Section 6 is a separate corner, but it raises the same question about preserving speed.

In a future session, I would like to try different lines with my instructor. After driving, I would use the recorded GPS data to set the same virtual timing lines before the corner and on the following straight, then compare several laps with similar entry speeds. No manual timing would be needed while driving. I would look at both exit speed and the time between those lines to see whether a faster exit actually improves the whole section. This would be a practical comparison rather than a fully controlled test, and I have not yet carried it out.

10. SYNTHESIZE — What the Comparisons Changed

The three comparisons gave me different questions. At the uphill U-turn, path and speed changed together. At the northwest U-turn, speed recovery differed while the GPS paths stayed close. At the final corner, a higher exit speed accompanied a quicker run to the finish, but an earlier upshift alone did not explain the result.

Together, these comparisons suggest that preserving speed through corners deserves further study. They also show why a recorded pattern is not enough to identify its cause. The next step is to test one question at a time.

11. NEXT TEST — Comparing Lines at a Common Entry Speed

The detailed path and speed comparison took place after driving. I have not yet tested the hypothesis under controlled driving conditions.

My next question is how different uphill U-turn paths affect exit speed when entry speed is held comparable. With my instructor, I would choose a common, repeatable entry speed suitable for both paths, compare several attempts, and record minimum speed, exit speed, section time and synchronized video under similar conditions.

I also expect each path to have its own feasible speed range. In a simplified steady turn, lateral acceleration demand is v²/r: a tighter radius requires more lateral acceleration at the same speed. With the same available lateral acceleration, a larger radius permits a higher speed. That is not a measured limit for this uphill corner, where gradient, braking, acceleration and grip also matter.

Comparing lines at a common entry speed is therefore different from exploring the limit of each line. I would start with the common-speed comparison and discuss any later progression with my instructor.
