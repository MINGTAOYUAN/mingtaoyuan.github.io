FULL STUDY / LAP COMPARISON & CORNER ANALYSIS

Data-Driven Racecraft

How My First Track Experience Led to a Telemetry Study

Mingtao Yuan · Buttonwillow Raceway Park · Mazda Miata NA 1.6 · April 20, 2026

The interactive charts above compare the morning and afternoon laps. The complete report below also discusses an intermediate late-morning lap and other telemetry channels. Sections 7 and 9 extend the study with northwest- and final-corner comparisons across all three session exports. Braking and throttle interpretations are hypotheses where direct pedal sensors were unavailable.

My role and support: I proposed the questions and interpretations described here. Cooper Hicks coached me on an experienced line through the uphill turn, and Hicks Motorsports provided the AiM data. AI tools helped visualize the recorded paths and show their speed and timing values. The later comparison helped me develop a hypothesis; I have not yet tested it in a controlled driving experiment.

The Question

My first track experience led to a question I explored in more detail after driving:

Could I use vehicle telemetry to understand why I was improving—and then use that understanding to make my driving better?

I had spent time practicing in racing simulators, but real driving immediately felt different. The physical forces, braking confidence, tire grip, and consequences of every decision were no longer simulated.

Instead of relying only on how a lap felt, I wanted to measure what was actually happening.

This report is a retrospective comparison and a plan for a future experiment.

1. OBSERVE — From Simulation to Reality

During training, I began to notice that top speed alone could not explain how quickly I completed a lap. Speed through the corners, especially at the exit, mattered too.

During my sessions at Buttonwillow Raceway Park, I noticed that I was becoming more comfortable with the car. I could brake with more confidence, carry more speed into some corners, and better anticipate how the car would respond.

But those were subjective impressions.

I wanted objective evidence.

So I began thinking about driving as a dynamic system rather than simply a sport.

The car generates measurable outputs—speed, acceleration, RPM, position, lateral acceleration—and my decisions as a driver influence those outputs.

After driving, I turned those observations into a question for the data:

How can vehicle telemetry be used to identify, quantify, and verify improvements in driving performance?

The driving was recorded with an AiM telemetry system; Hicks Motorsports provided the data used here.

3. ANALYZE — Finding the 7.401 Seconds

Between Lap A and Lap C, my lap time improved by:

7.401 seconds

or approximately:

4.67%

During training, I had already begun to question how much top speed explained. The later telemetry comparison gave me numbers to examine that idea.

My maximum GPS speed changed only from:

140.2 km/h → 142.3 km/h

But my average speed increased from:

105.4 km/h → 110.5 km/h

Even more interesting was minimum speed:

50.9 km/h → 61.3 km/h

This was one of the most important discoveries in the project.

The small change in maximum speed did not explain the improvement on its own. The corner-speed differences stood out, while exit speed and acceleration on the following straights remained part of the same question.

4. SPEED — Looking Beyond Top Speed

When I overlaid speed against distance, I could see that the fastest lap did not simply have a higher peak.

Instead, its speed trace remained higher through several important low-speed sections.

This gave numerical context to what I had noticed during training.

A car may spend only a short period near maximum speed, but every unnecessary reduction in corner speed affects not only the corner itself but also the acceleration zone that follows it.

I began to think less about “going faster” and more about preserving momentum.

During driving, I also noticed that reaching my usual upshift RPM sooner after a corner could help on the following straight. I mean reaching that RPM earlier in time through a better exit, not shifting at a lower RPM. The final-corner comparison in Section 9 examines that observation and why shift timing alone does not explain the result.

5. DELTA TIME — Locating Performance Loss

The analysis then compared cumulative time difference relative to my fastest selected lap.

This produced a delta-time trace.

Instead of saying:

“Lap C was 7.401 seconds faster.”

I could now ask:

“Where did those 7.401 seconds appear?”

The largest gains were concentrated in specific areas.

When I divided the approximately 4.64 km lap into ten equal-distance sectors, the first three sectors accounted for approximately:

4.86 seconds of improvement.

The final three sectors contributed approximately another:

2.27 seconds.

The middle of the lap changed relatively little.

In two sectors, my fastest overall lap was actually slightly slower.

That was important because it showed me that a personal-best lap is not necessarily an optimal lap.

There were still measurable weaknesses inside my fastest result.

How to read this chart

Across the surrounding analysis window, this represented approximately:

1.43 seconds of improvement over the 0.47–0.78 km window.

Similar gains appeared around other major speed minima.

They separate through the middle and exit: on Lap C, the recorded path follows a wider arc, reaching about 5 m outside the Lap A trace around 0.64–0.66 km, then converging again. The logger reports roughly 1 m positional accuracy, so this is an approximate comparison of vehicle paths, not a survey of the pavement edges.

Lap C enters the 0.47 km point at about 113 km/h versus 106 km/h on Lap A. Its lowest speed in the turn is about 61.3 km/h versus 50.9 km/h, and at 0.78 km it is about 99 km/h versus 92 km/h. Over the 0.47–0.78 km window, Lap C takes about 13.71 seconds against 15.14 seconds for Lap A—a gain of about 1.43 seconds. The GPS slope and within-lap altitude changes indicate a short uphill rise near the slowest point. Keeping more speed there may be especially useful as the car climbs, but the data do not establish that the wider path alone caused the gain: entry speed and unmeasured braking and throttle inputs also differed. I would test that hypothesis with synchronized video and comparable entry speeds.

After driving, the AI-assisted path and speed comparison helped me develop my hypothesis that the uphill exit speed was related to the path. My coach had guided the line during training; the later visualization made its recorded shape and speed differences easier to examine.

This also gave me a more specific way to think about the corner:

The fastest corner is not necessarily the corner with the latest braking point.

The entire sequence matters:

Entry → rotation → minimum speed → exit → acceleration

Optimizing only one part can damage another.

8. VEHICLE DYNAMICS — Learning to Use the Grip Envelope

Another measurable change appeared in lateral acceleration.

Peak absolute lateral acceleration increased from approximately:

1.09 g → 1.21 g

This did not mean that every corner should be driven at maximum lateral acceleration.

The higher measured peak showed a change in lateral acceleration. Without measuring the grip available in each session, it does not establish how close I was to the tire-road limit.

The stronger longitudinal deceleration was another measured change. My sense of becoming more comfortable comes from the driving experience, not from the acceleration values alone.

This introduced me to a concept I want to study more deeply:

The tire has a finite amount of available grip, and braking, cornering, and acceleration compete for that capability.

The northwest U-turn comparison gives me a concrete reason to study that transition: the speed recovery changed even though the recorded paths stayed close. It does not show how close either lap came to the grip limit.

Driving began to look less like a sequence of instincts and more like an applied vehicle-dynamics problem.

9. FINAL CORNER — Exit Speed, Shifting and the Run to the Finish

During training, I noticed that a good exit helped me reach my usual upshift RPM sooner on the following straight. I wanted to know whether the data supported that feeling. Reaching that RPM sooner is different from choosing to shift at a lower RPM.

Testing my first explanation

An AI-assisted review of all three AiM exports found 20 final-corner passages. After excluding three outlaps and one slow passage without the usual final three-to-four upshift, 16 complete laps could be compared. This extends the three selected laps used elsewhere in this report.

The shift is visible; its benefit is harder to separate

On afternoon L7, RPM falls from about 5,920 to 4,441 in roughly 0.40 seconds, accompanied by a calculated change from third to fourth gear. Speed briefly flattens around the shift before rising again. This identifies the event, but the RPM-drop duration is not a direct measurement of clutch operation.

L3 shifts about 0.38 seconds earlier than L2 and reaches the finish about 0.115 seconds sooner. That initially fits my explanation. But L3 also starts this section about 1.9 km/h faster, so the gain cannot be assigned to shifting alone.

L7 challenges a simple “earlier is faster” rule: it shifts about 0.54 seconds later than L3, yet finishes the section about 0.135 seconds sooner. It starts faster and reaches a higher RPM before shifting. Across the six complete afternoon laps, earlier shifting does not show a clear relationship with shorter section time.

What changed in my understanding

The more consistent finding is that a higher speed at the exit gate accompanies a shorter run to the finish. A better exit gives the car a higher starting speed on the straight; it does not necessarily produce a larger acceleration. These laps do not isolate the independent benefit of shift timing.

The next question starts before the exit

This brings me back to the line: how do entry position, the car’s direction and the path through a corner affect where I can begin sustained acceleration? The uphill U-turn in Section 6 is a separate corner, but it raises the same question about preserving speed.

In a future session, I would like to try different lines with my instructor. After driving, I would use the recorded GPS data to set the same virtual timing lines before the corner and on the following straight, then compare several laps with similar entry speeds. No manual timing would be needed while driving. I would look at both exit speed and the time between those lines to see whether a faster exit actually improves the whole section. This would be a practical comparison rather than a fully controlled test, and I have not yet carried it out.

10. HYPOTHESIZE — Turning Data into a Theory

After comparing the three laps, I developed a hypothesis:

Preserving speed through selected corners, especially at the exit, may explain an important part of the improvement. Better exits could also give the car a higher starting speed on the following straights. The maximum-speed comparison alone cannot separate those effects.

The observations that led me to this hypothesis were:

Maximum speed increased only about 1.5%.

Lap time improved about 4.7%.

The reported corner minimum speed increased about 20%.

Peak lateral acceleration increased about 12%.

These observations point to useful places to investigate. They do not isolate which driver input, path or condition caused the change.

11. NEXT TEST — Comparing Lines at a Common Entry Speed

The detailed path and speed comparison took place after driving. I have not yet tested the hypothesis under controlled driving conditions.

My next question is how different uphill U-turn paths affect exit speed when entry speed is held comparable. With my instructor, I would choose a common, repeatable entry speed suitable for both paths, compare several attempts, and record minimum speed, exit speed, section time and synchronized video under similar conditions.

I also expect each path to have its own feasible speed range. In a simplified steady turn, lateral acceleration demand is v²/r: a tighter radius requires more lateral acceleration at the same speed. With the same available lateral acceleration, a larger radius permits a higher speed. That is not a measured limit for this uphill corner, where gradient, braking, acceleration and grip also matter.

Comparing lines at a common entry speed is therefore different from exploring the limit of each line. I would start with the common-speed comparison and discuss any later progression with my instructor.

The feedback loop I want to complete is:

Drive → Measure → Analyze → Plan a change → Test again

THE PROCESS CONTINUES

Measure. Question. Test again.
