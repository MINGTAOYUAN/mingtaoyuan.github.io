FEATURED INSIGHT / BUTTONWILLOW · APRIL 20, 2026

One Corner.
Two Lines.

What two laps through an uphill U-turn taught me about speed, trajectory, and the limits of what telemetry can explain.

I wanted to understand something more specific than my overall lap time: why was I carrying more speed through one of Buttonwillow's slowest sections in the afternoon than in the morning?

01 / The corner

The uphill U-turn is a useful place to study this question because a driver has to slow the car, make it rotate, and then carry momentum through the climb and out of the turn. I had been learning braking points and racing lines with instruction, but comparing laps made me curious about what my actual path and speed showed.

The comparison below uses two selected laps recorded with AiM GPS telemetry on April 20, 2026. Lap A is from the morning, and Lap C is from the afternoon. They were recorded in different sessions, so the comparison is a starting point rather than a controlled test.

02 / Two recorded paths

On the approach, the two GPS traces are close together. Through the middle of the turn and toward the exit, Lap C takes a wider recorded arc, about five meters outside Lap A near 0.64–0.66 km before the paths come together again. GPS position accuracy was reported at roughly one meter, so these are approximate trajectories, not exact surveyed racing lines.

03 / What changed in the data?

50.6 → 61.3Minimum GPS speed, km/h · Lap A → C
15.14 → 13.71Time through the 0.47–0.78 km section, seconds
1.43 sApproximate improvement in this section

Lap C entered the comparison window faster too: about 113 km/h rather than 106 km/h at 0.47 km. Near 0.78 km, its speed was about 99 km/h instead of 92 km/h. The gain was not just about the minimum speed at the tightest part of the corner; it involved the entry, middle, and exit together.

04 / What I think it means

The wider and apparently smoother recorded arc on Lap C may have helped me preserve momentum, especially on the uphill portion. But I cannot say that the line alone saved 1.43 seconds. My entry speed also differed, and the data did not include direct brake pressure or throttle-position measurements.

That distinction matters to me. The GPS trace tells me where the two laps differ. To understand why, I need to consider the video, driver inputs, and conditions as well. A faster lap can suggest a better approach without proving it.

05 / The next experiment

If I could repeat the comparison, I would aim for similar entry speeds and track conditions, record synchronized video, and vary one aspect of the line at a time. I would compare entry, minimum, and exit speeds over several attempts instead of drawing a conclusion from only two laps.

This is a learning case study, not a claim that the wider line is always the fastest. The observations are drawn from AiM GPS data; interpretations of braking and throttle remain hypotheses without pedal sensors. Data provided by Hicks Motorsports; analysis guided by Cooper Hicks.
